\begin{abstract}
The purpose of this work is to discuss and illustrate the need of seeding Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms when dealing with unobserved variables, as well as to propose a seeding procedure that relies on a distance-based approach for such cases.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In real-world scenarios, we often aim to explain a set of traits of a phenomenon of interest under specific conditions. From a probabilistic perspective, we usually propose a stochastic mechanism to approximate the underlying law that is governing the phenomenon of interest in such a way that the outcomes of such a stochastic mechanism can be regarded as realisations of the set of traits, say $\pi(\mathbf{z} \vert \boldsymbol{\theta})$, where $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is an unobservable random vector that fully defines the proposed stochastic mechanism.\\

The aforementioned proposal is the core of Statistics, a probabilistic framework for drawing statements about the set of traits by leveraging available measurements $\mathbf{z}_{1}$, $\mathbf{z}_{2}$, $\ldots$, $\mathbf{z}_{N}$ of them, here denoted by $\mathbf{z}^{obs}$. In this context, there are two common approaches. On the one hand, the frequentist approach focuses on estimating $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, say $\boldsymbol{\widehat{\theta}}$. On the other hand, the Bayesian approach \citep{BS1994} requires the elicitation of $\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, also known as the prior distribution, but returns (via Bayes' rule) $\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta} \vert \mathbf{z}^{obs})$, also known as the posterior distribution.\\

\section{MCMC in practice}
\label{sec:background}

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods \citep{HMCMC2011} are widely used in practice for reconstructing posterior distributions. These methods rely on the theoretical principle of defining a homogeneous, Harris-recurrent, and aperiodic Markov chain whose stationary probability density function is the target posterior distribution; that is, to construct a suitable Markov chain $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}, \tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(2)}, \ldots$ such that $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(t)} \underset{t \to \infty}{\sim} \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta} \vert \mathbf{z}_{1:N})$. \section{Seeding MCMC algorithms}
\label{sec:alg}
 
\subsection{Motivation}

A more challenging scenario is when, despite having a well-defined stochastic mechanism, the set of traits is comprised of unobservable and observable variables, that is, if $\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ we only have measurements for $\mathbf{y}$, say $\mathbf{y}^{obs}$. At first sight this might seem far-fetched, however, it is precisely the setting of state-space models, where the state vector is unobservable and the output vector is observable. In this case, we instead seek to reconstruct $\pi(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\theta} \vert \mathbf{y}^{obs})$, and thus, we have not only initialise $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ but also $\mathbf{x}$. Built upon a distance-based approach, we proposed the following seeding process for MCMC algorithms when dealing with unobserved variables.

\subsection{Algorithm}

Given a prior distribution for $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, $\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, we start by drawing a sample from it, say $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}$. Next, we simulate a realisation of $\pi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \vert \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})$, say $(\mathbf{x}^{(t)}, \mathbf{y}^{(t)})$, which can be regarded as a candidate measurement of the full set of traits $\mathbf{z}$. Now, let $d:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ be a distance function and $d^{max}$ a distance threshold. We can argument that the candidate measurement $\mathbf{y}^{(t)}$ is at the bulk of $\pi(\mathbf{y})$ if $d(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}, \mathbf{y}^{obs}) < d^{max}$. In that case, we can also argument that the candidate measurement $\mathbf{x}^{(t)}$ is at the bulk of $\pi(\mathbf{x})$. We thus have a potential initial value for the MCMC algorithm that can help us with reconstructing $\pi(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\theta} \vert \mathbf{y}^{obs})$, namely, $(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}, \mathbf{x}^{(t)})$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:inits} summaries such a seeding process.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Distance-based seeding process}
\label{alg:inits}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\Require (Set) $d^{max}$
\Require $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}$
\Ensure $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}, \mathbf{x}^{(t)}$

\State $t = 0$

\Repeat
	\State $t \leftarrow t+1$
	\State $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)} \sim \pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})$
    \State $\mathbf{x}^{(t)}, \mathbf{y}^{(t)} \sim \pi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \vert \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})$
    
\Until{$d(\mathbf{y}^{(t)}, \mathbf{y}^{obs}) < d^{max}$}

\Return $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}, \mathbf{x}^{(t)}$

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
